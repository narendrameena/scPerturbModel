# Six papers read 2026-08-31 — and what each does to this project

Detailed readings, each ending with what it changes for us. Two of them are
consequential: one caps the ceiling of the framing I had just proposed, and one
undermines an assumption the whole project rests on.

---

## 1. Sadowski et al. 2024, *Cell Genomics* 4:100722 — "Characterizing the genetic architecture of drug response using gene-context interaction methods"

**Design.** 342,257 unrelated white British individuals in UK Biobank. Four
drugs, chosen for prescription frequency: statins (56,169 users vs 286,088
non-users), metformin (8,606), warfarin (3,753), methotrexate (1,865). For each,
both primary-effect and side-effect phenotypes (Table 1) — e.g. statins → LDL
cholesterol and CVD (primary), glucose / HbA1c / T2D (side effects).

**Two methods.**
- **h²_response**, a new quantity: the SNP heritability of the *phenotype change
  after treatment*. Estimated by post-processing **GxEMM**, which explicitly
  models treatment-dependent heteroscedasticity — the authors stress this is
  essential for unbiased treatment-dependent heritability. Aggregates
  genome-wide, so it is powerful but names no genes.
- **TxEWAS**, also new: a GxE extension of TWAS. Genetically imputes expression,
  then tests whether imputed expression *interacts* with the treatment variable.
  Heteroscedasticity handled with a sandwich variance estimator. Gains
  interpretability (candidate causal genes) and power (aggregates SNPs, fewer
  tests).

**Endogeneity is treated as a first-class problem.** Treatment assignment depends
on baseline phenotype — sick people get statins — which biases every GxE model.
They characterise it with theory, simulations and extra data, and validate the
gene–statin interactions on LDL in an independent retrospective longitudinal
pharmacogenomic study.

**Results.**

| response phenotype | h²_response | h²_hom (untreated) |
|---|---|---|
| statin → LDL cholesterol | **9%** | 21% |
| statin → HbA1c | **10%** | 29% |
| statin → blood glucose | **11%** | 11% |
| metformin → BMI | **17%** | 28% |
| metformin → LDL | 2% | 11% |
| warfarin, methotrexate | not significant (underpowered) | — |

Dozens of genes reach significance (statin–LDL: LPL, PCSK9, FADS1, SIK1,
TMEM258…; statin–A1c: TCF7L2, GIPR, PSRC1, HLA-DQB1/2…). PGS accuracy varies up
to **2-fold** with treatment status, so standard PGSs will underperform in
clinical settings where many patients are treated.

**What it does to us.** This is precisely the "genetic architecture of drug
response" reframing I proposed as our Nature Genetics route — already executed,
in humans, at biobank scale, with two new methods and independent replication.
**And it was published in Cell Genomics, not Nature Genetics.** That is the
sharpest available calibration of the ceiling for this framing. It also gives us
the field's benchmark: genome-wide common variation explains roughly **9–17%** of
drug response. Our numbers (a single allele explains median 5.5% of the
interaction; aggregate genotype R² near zero) sit consistently below that, in a
system with 738 contexts rather than 342,257 individuals.

## 2. Ben-David et al. 2018, *Nature* 560:325 — "Genetic and transcriptional evolution alters cancer cell line drug response"

**The most consequential of the six for this project.**

**Cross-laboratory comparison.** 106 cell lines whole-exome sequenced by *both*
CCLE (Broad) and GDSC (Sanger), reanalysed through one pipeline:

- germline variant allelic fractions agree almost perfectly, **median r = 0.95**
- somatic variants agree far less, **median r = 0.86** (p < 2×10⁻¹⁶)
- **a median of 19% of detected non-silent mutations appear in only one of the
  two datasets** (range 10–90%)
- 26% of genes with copy-number alterations are discordant (range 7–99%)
- a median of 22% of the genome is subclonal across 916 CCLE lines

**Within-line diversification.** 27 strains of MCF7 (19 untreated, 7 with
"neutral" modifications, 1 in-vivo passaged outlier excluded). Ten chromosome
arms — 25% of the genome — differentially gained or lost between strains. Of 283
gained and 405 lost genes, only 13% of gains and 21% of losses were shared by all
strains. Barcoding showed the mechanism is positive clonal selection, highly
sensitive to culture conditions. Similar results in 13 further cell lines.

**Drug response.** 321 compounds at 5 µM across the 27 MCF7 strains. 55 had
strong activity (>50% inhibition) in at least one strain — and **48 of those 55
(87%) were completely inactive (<20% inhibition) in at least one other strain**.
At a stricter threshold, 33 of 42 (79%). All 33 validated in 8-point dose–response
(median Spearman ρ = 0.42 between screens, p = 3×10⁻⁹).

**It is not assay noise**, and they prove it four ways: replicate treatments
r = 0.97; compounds sharing a mechanism share activity patterns (p = 3×10⁻⁷ —
three proteasome inhibitors behave alike, and biochemically measured proteasome
activity differs accordingly); 82% of differentially active compounds show
mechanism-consistent expression differences between sensitive and resistant
strains (p = 2×10⁻⁵); proliferation rate explains almost none of it
(median ρ = 0.017, p = 0.60). Specific links are recovered — PTEN inactivation →
AKT-inhibitor sensitivity, ESR1 loss → tamoxifen resistance.

**What it does to us — three things, one of which changes an analysis in flight.**

1. **It supplies the mechanism for our cross-dataset non-replication.** We found
   the mechanism ranking does not transfer (Tahoe vs LINCS ρ = +0.09; LINCS vs
   PRISM ρ = −0.09 over 67 classes) and attributed it to readout differences.
   Ben-David offers a simpler, measured explanation: *the same nominal cell line
   is genetically different between laboratories.* Tahoe, LINCS, PRISM and GDSC
   each used their own strains.
2. **It adds a second explanation for our genotype negatives.** We concluded they
   were a power ceiling (§10–11). But we join CCLE genotype to PRISM/GDSC
   response, and 19% of non-silent mutations are dataset-specific — so the
   genotype–phenotype link is attenuated by measurement mismatch before power
   even enters. Both explanations are live; we have been asserting only one.
3. **It biases the analysis currently running.** `genetic_architecture.py`
   compares synonymous variants (confounding only) against nonsynonymous ones
   (mechanism + confounding). Ben-David shows germline calls are far more
   reproducible than somatic ones (r = 0.95 vs 0.86). Synonymous variants are
   largely germline, so they carry **less measurement error** than the
   nonsynonymous somatic block. The comparison is therefore *conservative*: if
   nonsynonymous still beats synonymous, the mechanistic signal is real; if they
   tie, the result is ambiguous rather than a clean negative. This has to be
   stated in the interpretation.

It also shows the topic reaches *Nature* — but with new experimental data: 27
strains sequenced, profiled and screened.

## 3. Jack, Rotroff & Motsinger-Reif 2014, *Curr Mol Med* — "Cell line models of drug response: successes and lessons from this pharmacogenomic model"

Review of lymphoblastoid cell lines (LCLs) as a pharmacogenomic system.
Advantages: public genome-wide data (CEPH pedigrees, HapMap, 1000 Genomes) means
only the phenotyping must be paid for; scalable to large n; far fewer design
confounders than clinical trials. Statistical focus on association mapping —
multivariate concentration–response modelling, multiple-testing burden, the
"triangle model" for candidate variants, then siRNA knockdown for functional
confirmation. Family-based panels permit heritability estimation of drug
response; population-based panels give finer mapping resolution.

**What it does to us.** It names, as a central and long-known pitfall of
cell-line pharmacogenomics, exactly the confound that killed our MUC16 candidate:
**population stratification** — systematic allele-frequency differences between
ancestries combined with group-level differences in dose response. Our §15
germline finding is not an idiosyncratic catch; it is the failure mode this
literature warns about. Worth citing as such rather than presenting as novel.

## 4. Wang et al. 2022, *NAR* 50:D1164 — "CeDR Atlas: a knowledgebase of cellular drug response"

Combines CMap drug-induced expression signatures with 582 single-cell datasets to
*infer* cellular drug response for hundreds of cell types: ~140 phenotypes, 1,250
tissue–cell combinations, human/mouse/cell lines, searchable by drug, cell type,
tissue, disease or signature gene.

**What it does to us.** Prior art on the phrase "cellular drug response at
single-cell resolution", so the framing is partly occupied. But CeDR *infers*
response by signature matching against bulk CMap; it never measures a
perturbation in a single cell. Our work measures directly and decomposes
variance. The distinction is real and should be stated explicitly in any
positioning, rather than discovered by a reviewer.

## 5. Zhao et al., *Cell Syst.* — "Large-scale characterization of drug responses of clinically relevant proteins in cancer cell lines"

RPPA profiling of ~210 clinically relevant proteins across **>12,000 cancer cell
line samples** in response to ~170 compounds. Perturbed protein responses add
substantial predictive power for drug sensitivity beyond baseline; a systematic
protein–drug connectivity map is built; interactive portal released.

**What it does to us.** Two direct connections. First, it supplies a **third
readout** — protein — for the decoupling result in §12, where transcriptional and
viability context-dependence proved uncorrelated (ρ = −0.09 over 67 classes).
Protein response is the natural arbiter of whether that decoupling is
readout-specific or general, and the data are public. Second, "perturbed
responses predict better than baseline" is the same phenomenon as our few-shot
result (§6), where measuring ~20 arbitrary compounds in a new line beat every
baseline descriptor. That is independent support from a different modality.

## 6. Schlüter & Schönhuth 2025, arXiv:2507.02818 — "Genetic features for drug responses in cancer: an ensemble feature-selection approach"

Ensemble of ML models (SVR, linear, ridge) relating GDSC cell-line genetic and
transcriptomic features to IC50. Reduces 38,977 features to 421. Reports a
strong *linear* relationship between genetic features and response — complex
non-linear models are not required. Key claims: **CNVs are more predictive than
mutations**; mutation data, though central to cancer development, is of lesser
importance for response prediction; and cancer driver genes are only partially
useful for predicting response.

**What it does to us.** Independent, GDSC-based support for what our own
architecture analysis is showing: driver mutations explain little of drug
response. It also flags an omission — **copy-number variation is a predictor
block we have not used anywhere**, and this paper claims it outperforms
mutations. If we run the variance partition again, CNV belongs in it.

## Consolidated consequences

**For positioning.** The genetic-architecture reframing is capped: Sadowski did
it better, in humans, and landed in Cell Genomics. Nature Genetics via this route
is not realistic.

**For our claims.** Ben-David requires two edits to what we currently assert:
the genotype negatives have a second explanation (cross-lab genotype mismatch,
19% of non-silent mutations) alongside power, and the cross-dataset
non-replication of the mechanism ranking has a simpler candidate cause than
readout biology — strain divergence.

**For what to run next.** Ben-David also points at the strongest remaining novel
analysis, and one only we are positioned to do. He showed strain divergence
changes drug response in *one* cell line (MCF7, 27 strains, one dose, viability).
We hold four datasets — Tahoe, LINCS, PRISM, GDSC — that share nominal cell
lines. The question "**how much of the line-specific drug response attributed to
cell-line identity actually reproduces across laboratories?**" is well-powered,
unanswered at scale, and directly actionable for everyone who uses these atlases.
Adding CNV (Schlüter) and protein response (Zhao) as further blocks is cheap and
both datasets are public.
